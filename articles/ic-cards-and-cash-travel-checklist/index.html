Quick answer: the safest IC card and cash setup

For a first Japan trip, use both an IC card and cash. An IC card such as Suica, PASMO, Welcome Suica, ICOCA, or another interoperable regional card is the easiest way to handle many local trains, subways, buses, convenience stores, vending machines, and small everyday purchases. Cash is still the backup for physical-card charging, small shops, rural transport, shrine or temple visits, older restaurants, coin lockers that do not accept your card, and moments when an overseas card or mobile wallet fails.

If you land in Tokyo and want a simple temporary visitor option, check current Welcome Suica availability first. JR East pages checked on July 18, 2026 describe Welcome Suica as deposit-free, usable for travel and shopping, and valid for 28 days from the purchase date. If you prefer PASMO, Tokyo Metro's PASMO page checked on the same date describes PASMO as a prepaid card usable for subway, buses, stores, vending machines, and many interoperable railway and bus member shops nationwide; it also states that a new PASMO card includes a 500 yen deposit.

Do not plan a cashless-only trip. JNTO's credit-card guidance checked on July 18, 2026 says international cards are generally accepted, but not always for short-distance train or subway fares, and it points visitors to Japan Post Bank and Seven Bank ATMs for cash withdrawal. The best setup is one primary payment method, one IC card or mobile IC setup, and enough yen for the first day and any rural or low-tech stops.

Who this IC card and cash guide is for

Use this page if you are visiting Japan for the first time and want to avoid payment surprises at station gates, buses, vending machines, local restaurants, lockers, and airport arrivals. It is written for travelers who hear "just use Suica" but still need to know when cash matters, which card type to check, and how to build a backup that works outside central Tokyo.

This is not a live inventory page for physical cards, mobile wallet compatibility, or every regional transport operator. Card sales, app compatibility, refund rules, and charging methods can change. Use this guide to choose a payment strategy, then verify the exact card product, airport counter, ticket machine, and phone setup on official sources before departure and again on arrival day.

Step-by-step action plan

Step 1: before departure, decide whether you will use a mobile IC setup or buy a physical card in Japan. If you choose mobile, test the wallet setup, funding card, and phone battery plan before leaving home. If you choose physical, check current official sales information for the airport or first major station you will actually use.

Step 2: plan cash for the first 24 hours. Arrivals are when payment friction hurts most. You may need yen for an IC top-up, a bus, food after a late landing, luggage storage, a small shop, or a card machine that rejects your overseas payment. JNTO currency guidance lists Japan Post Bank and Seven Bank ATM access for overseas-issued cards; still, check your own bank fees and withdrawal limits before travel.

Step 3: charge the card before high-friction days. Do not wait until the ticket gate rejects you during a transfer. Tokyo Metro's PASMO page lists ticket machines, station offices, and pass offices as charging places, and JR East's Welcome Suica materials describe cash charging and a 20,000 yen maximum stored value for the card. Use those figures as official checks, then follow the machine or counter instructions in front of you.

Step 4: separate local transport from long-distance rail. An IC card is excellent for many local rides and small purchases, but it is not a magic replacement for Shinkansen seat reservations, airport limited-express decisions, rail pass eligibility, or every rural bus. Use the IC card to reduce everyday friction, then handle long-distance tickets with the operator or pass product that fits the route.

Step 5: before leaving Japan, spend or refund intentionally. Tourist cards, regular cards, and mobile products can have different balance, deposit, refund, and validity rules. Do not load a large balance on the final day unless you know whether you can use or recover it.

Cash checklist and payment backup

Carry enough yen to solve small problems without searching for an ATM under pressure. That does not mean carrying a huge amount of cash. It means having usable bills and coins for the first meal, a local bus, a locker, a temple or shrine stop, a small restaurant, a vending machine, and a top-up if your card balance runs low.

JNTO's currency page checked on July 18, 2026 lists Japanese banknotes and coins, notes that a coin purse is practical, and explains that customs declaration is required when entering or leaving Japan with more than one million yen in cash, checks, traveler's checks, securities, and similar items. Most travelers will be far below that threshold, but the rule is useful because it reminds you not to treat cash planning casually.

Your backup should have layers: a primary credit card, a second card kept separately, an IC card or mobile IC setup, and yen for the situations where cards are not accepted. If your phone wallet is the main IC tool, keep battery reserve and know how you will ride if the phone is dead. If a physical card is the main tool, keep enough cash to recharge it.

Where IC cards work and where they fail

IC cards are strongest in dense travel days: tap through many local train or subway gates, pay for small convenience-store items, use compatible vending machines, and avoid buying individual local tickets. GO TOKYO says IC cards can be used on many major rail and bus services in metropolitan Tokyo and many other parts of Japan, and that they are prepaid and rechargeable. That is the everyday convenience travelers want.

The failure points are usually edge cases. Some buses, regional lines, older shops, small restaurants, market stalls, temples, coin lockers, or ticket products may not accept the card you expect. Some transfers require enough fare to exit or transfer correctly. Some cards can be bought or refunded only in the issuing region. Some mobile setups depend on your phone, card issuer, and app region. The answer is not to panic; it is to carry cash and verify the exact route when leaving major cities.

Common mistakes

The first mistake is assuming one payment method works everywhere. Japan is increasingly cashless, but visitors still run into short-distance fare gaps, small shops, rural buses, and machines that prefer cash. The second mistake is buying or charging a physical card without knowing the remaining-balance and refund rules, especially on a short trip.

The third mistake is sharing one IC card across a group. Each traveler needs a clean tap-in and tap-out record, so one card passed around at gates creates confusion. The fourth mistake is ignoring the first hour after arrival. That is exactly when you need money, transport, data, luggage, and sleep to work together.

The fifth mistake is treating Tokyo advice as nationwide advice. Tokyo, Kansai, Hokkaido, Kyushu, Okinawa, rural bus routes, and island travel can behave differently. The sixth mistake is loading too much money onto a tourist card near the end of the trip. Keep balances useful, not trapped.

What to verify on official sources

Verify current card sales, card type, validity, deposit, refund, charging method, maximum stored value, mobile compatibility, and route acceptance on official sources. Start with JR East for Suica and Welcome Suica, Tokyo Metro or PASMO sources for PASMO, JNTO for national visitor context, and the local railway or bus operator for any rural or regional route.

Also verify ATM locations and overseas-card compatibility before you leave the airport area. JNTO points travelers toward Japan Post Bank and Seven Bank ATMs, but your own bank can still add fees, block a transaction, or set a daily limit. If the trip includes late arrivals, islands, ski towns, hiking areas, or small inns, check payment and cash access before the travel day.

  • Check whether your arrival airport or first station sells the card you want.
  • Check whether a mobile IC setup works with your phone, card issuer, and region settings.
  • Check how to charge the card and whether cash is required for your physical card.
  • Check refund and remaining-balance rules before loading a large amount.
  • Check regional bus and private-rail acceptance before leaving major city routes.

FAQ

Do tourists still need cash in Japan?

Yes. IC cards and credit cards are useful, but cash remains important for top-ups, smaller shops, rural buses, temples, lockers, older restaurants, and payment failures.

Is Welcome Suica good for tourists?

It can be a good short-trip option if current sales locations fit your arrival. JR East describes it as deposit-free, usable for travel and shopping, and valid for 28 days from purchase.

Should I get Suica or PASMO?

For many Tokyo travelers either can work because interoperability is broad. Choose the product you can actually buy or set up, then verify deposit, refund, validity, and charging rules.

Can I use an IC card everywhere in Japan?

No. JNTO says regional cards can make travel and small purchases easier nationwide, but individual buses, rural lines, shops, ticket products, and machines can still be exceptions.

Can I use a credit card instead of an IC card?

Not reliably for every local fare. JNTO says international cards are generally accepted, but not always for short-distance train or subway fares. Keep an IC or cash option.

How much can I load onto PASMO or Welcome Suica?

Tokyo Metro lists PASMO charging up to 20,000 yen, and JR East Welcome Suica materials also describe a 20,000 yen stored-value limit. Check the current machine or official page before loading a large amount.

Can one IC card be shared by two travelers?

Not for the same gated trip. Each traveler needs their own tap-in and tap-out record. Sharing one card creates fare and gate problems.

When should I recheck official IC card information?

Recheck before departure, again at the arrival airport or first major station, and anytime your route leaves major cities for rural buses, private railways, islands, or small towns.
